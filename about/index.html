About LinkedIn

I'm Jason Harper, an economist and founder based in the Detroit area. Most of my career has been spent at the same intersection: taking analytical work — forecasting, data science, and now AI-built software — and turning it into businesses that hold up under real-world pressure.

My training is in economics. I hold a BA and MA in Economics along with an MBA, and that background still shapes how I work. Economics teaches you to be suspicious of clean narratives, to ask what the data actually supports, and to think in systems rather than anecdotes. Those habits turn out to be just as useful for building companies as for building models.

I got my start in the data world as a Domo partner, building an analytics consultancy on that platform and later selling the business. That experience taught me two things: that most organizations struggle less with collecting data than with acting on it, and that a services firm is only as good as the outcomes it can point to.

In 2016 I founded RXA, a data science firm in Ann Arbor. We grew it into a team doing applied data science and analytics work for enterprise clients, and in 2023 RXA was acquired by OneMagnify, where I serve as Founder / Managing Director. Along the way I also founded Ready Signal, where I'm President — an external-data platform born from a recurring problem we kept hitting in forecasting work: the signal you need is often outside your own four walls, and assembling it by hand is slow and error-prone.

Today I run VYGO, a production engineering firm for AI-built software. AI tools have made it remarkably easy to get to a working prototype — and remarkably easy to mistake that prototype for a product. VYGO takes software that already works in a demo and rebuilds the layer underneath it so it survives real users, security reviews, and day-to-day operations: authentication, data handling, error paths, deployment, the unglamorous machinery that decides whether something ships or stalls.

On the side, I build Design Parenting, a product that applies the same systems thinking to how a family actually runs — treating the household as something you can design deliberately rather than improvise indefinitely.

If there's a through-line, it's this: I like the gap between something that works once and something that works reliably. Closing that gap — in a forecast, a data pipeline, or a piece of software — is where I've done my best work, and it's where I expect to keep spending my time.
